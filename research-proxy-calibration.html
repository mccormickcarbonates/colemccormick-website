Research

Experimental Proxy Calibration

Using high-temperature, replacement dolomitization experiments to test and correct the geochemical proxies that we use to reconstruct the paleoredox conditions of ancient seawater.

Overview

Dolomite forms a significant proportion of ancient carbonate rocks throughout Earth history, yet it rarely forms under near-surface, low-temperature conditions in modern sedimentary systems. This apparent contradiction, known as “the dolomite problem,” is a real obstacle for geochemistry — many proxies that are used to reconstruct the geochemical composition of ancient seawater are measured in rocks that have undergone some degree of diagenetic alteration, and it is often unclear whether the original seawater signal survived.

To get a controlled answer, I react calcium carbonate powder with a magnesium-rich fluid of known composition in sealed vessels at elevated temperature and pressure, watching it progressively convert to dolomite over hours to weeks via a poorly-ordered precursor phase. Because the fluid and mineral compositions are fixed, any trace element or isotope measured in the product can be directly attributed to how it partitioned during the reaction, not inferred from a sample of unknown history.

The current focus is uranium. Uranium isotopes (δ238U) in carbonate rocks are among the best tools for reconstructing the oxygenation history of Earth's oceans, since the 238U/235U ratio shifts predictably with seafloor anoxia. But that proxy is almost always measured in rocks that have experienced some dolomitization, and it wasn't known whether the process preserves or resets the original signal.

Our results show that U(VI) is strongly rock-buffered during dolomitization: dolomite's uranium concentration is controlled mainly by how much uranium was available in the parent carbonate, not by the fluid passing through it. That partitioning behavior, quantified here for the first time, provides the quantitative foundation needed to interpret the δ238U paleoredox proxy in ancient dolostones. This program, developed with Dr. Kimberly V. Lau and collaborators at Penn State and Michigan State, is expanding to calibrate additional proxies, including dolomite as an archive of seawater sulfate (δ34S) and a dual clumped-isotope (Δ47/Δ48) paleothermometer, with mentees Watts Dietrich and Isaac Bondzie-Selby.

That distinction matters beyond this one proxy. If uranium is rock-buffered while others, like strontium and iodine, are fluid-buffered, no single diagenetic correction applies uniformly across the geologic record. Each proxy must be calibrated on its own terms before it can be trusted to reconstruct ancient ocean chemistry, the ground-truthing this program is built to provide.
