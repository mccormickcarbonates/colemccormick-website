Research

Experimental Rock Mechanics

Testing how rocks actually break under pressure — the fracture mechanics that ultimately control where fluids can flow underground.

Overview

Fluid flow through the subsurface — whether the hydrothermal fluids that form dolomite, or fluids in engineered and natural reservoirs more broadly — is fundamentally controlled by fractures, and fractures are governed by rock mechanics. During my Ph.D., in collaboration with Dr. Ernest H. Rutter at The University of Manchester, I ran triaxial deformation experiments on Carrara marble and Solnhofen limestone — two well-characterized reference rock types — to test for the existence of “hybrid failure,” a debated fracture mode that combines features of both shear fracturing and extensional (tensile) cracking.

By systematically varying confining pressure, this work mapped out where hybrid fractures occur relative to the more familiar pure-shear and pure-extension failure modes, helping resolve a long-standing question in structural geology and rock mechanics. This experimental grounding in how rocks fracture directly informs my interpretation of the fault-controlled fluid pathways studied in my hydrothermal dolomitization research.
